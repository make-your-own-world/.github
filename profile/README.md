## What's Broken

Some people are exceptionally attuned to particular aspects of the world. Images, sounds, chemistry, mathematics, engineering, art, language, critical analysis, or whatever makes an individual tick. They may notice patterns others overlook, understand complex relationships, or spend years exploring something that deeply interests them.

These abilities can be extraordinary, but they don't always fit conventional expectations. Intense focus and unusual interests are sometimes treated as problems to manage rather than understood in terms of what they mean to the individual. Something that brings a person happiness, purpose, or a deeper understanding of the world isn't inherently unproductive simply because others don't recognize its value.

Being exceptionally good at something also doesn't mean having the resources to pursue it. Developing an idea takes time, equipment, funding, planning, and often skills unrelated to the work itself. Someone capable of making an important discovery may have neither the interest nor the ability to market themselves, manage a business, or compete for public attention.

We tend to assume that valuable work will eventually attract support. In practice, visibility and usefulness are different things. An idea can be important to someone, or potentially useful to thousands of others, without ever attracting an audience or financial backing.

There is a deeper contradiction here. People exchange their time and energy for money, partly to gain the freedom to spend more of their lives doing what matters to them. Yet those same activities are often dismissed as unproductive unless they generate income. Whether an interest can pay someone's bills is a practical concern, not a measure of its worth.

Established institutions, professional disciplines, and funding programs offer valuable knowledge and resources, but they also operate within their own priorities. Even fields studied for generations contain unresolved problems. Experience and expertise should support further exploration, not determine who is allowed to contribute or which questions are considered settled.

Much of the knowledge people do create is also scattered across disconnected systems. Research, software, conversations, observations, and the reasoning behind decisions can become separated from their original context. Sometimes the final result survives while everything that explains how it came to exist disappears.

Human time and energy are finite. Once spent, they cannot be recovered. The knowledge and experience produced through them shouldn't be lost simply because they weren't commercially successful, widely recognized, or preserved by a particular service.

## Freedom to Flourish

People experience and understand the world in different ways. What makes everyday life challenging for one person may also be connected to an extraordinary ability to recognize patterns, explore ideas, create, or understand something deeply.

Rather than measuring everyone against the same expectations, there is an opportunity to build environments that accommodate those differences and allow people to pursue what brings them happiness, meaning, and purpose.

Support should help individuals develop their abilities and live fulfilling lives on their own terms. An unconventional interest or way of thinking doesn't need to be changed simply because it doesn't fit a conventional definition of productivity.

Human creativity and curiosity take countless forms. With the freedom to explore, the tools to preserve what they discover, and ways to connect with others who share their interests, individual pursuits can become valuable contributions without requiring commercial success or public recognition.

**The goal isn't to make everyone fit the same world. It's to give individuals the means to make more of their own.**

## Tools to Fix It

Make Your Own World develops, assembles, and catalogs open-source software, research, and practical tools that support this approach.

The architecture is **local-first, self-hostable, offline-capable, interoperable, and distributed**. People should be able to preserve and work with their information independently, using the tools and systems they choose.

The work includes:

- **Knowledge preservation:** Keeping original documents, conversations, research, experiences, decisions, and unsuccessful attempts, along with the context connecting them.
- **Semantic understanding and discovery:** Connecting related ideas, concepts, and contributions through meaning rather than popularity, filenames, or keywords alone.
- **Independent automation:** Using interchangeable tools and computational models to organize, process, and develop information.
- **Distributed collaboration:** Allowing independently maintained systems to discover and exchange contributions with their owners' permission.
- **Research and reproducibility:** Preserving evidence, methods, experiments, results, and failures so others can evaluate and extend them.
- **Publishing and communication:** Making work accessible through portable formats without tying its continued existence to a particular service.

These capabilities are intended to work together, but individual components should remain useful on their own.

The emphasis is on practical progress. Existing knowledge deserves respect, but accepted methods and established institutions aren't the final boundaries of discovery. Contributions should be evaluated through their substance, evidence, and results, not merely the credentials, popularity, or affiliations of their creators.

## Independent Knowledge, Connected Worlds

The longer-term goal is a distributed, decentralized foundation for creating, preserving, discovering, and exchanging knowledge.

Today, knowledge is scattered across publications, repositories, institutions, and personal archives. Paywalls, language barriers, unfamiliar terminology, and disappearing websites make valuable work difficult to find, even when it could save someone months of effort. The problem is not always that answers don't exist, but that people cannot reach or connect them.

The aim is to separate knowledge from the systems used to manage it. Projects should retain their history, including failed attempts. Publications should retain their sources and context. Conversations should preserve the ideas and decisions behind them. Tools, providers, and models should be replaceable without losing that information.

Individuals and communities maintain their own collections, under their own control, and choose what to share. Those collections can connect across languages, disciplines, and technologies, making related work easier to discover, reference, test, and build upon.

No central repository, institution, or model needs to decide what everyone preserves or accepts. Provenance keeps contributions traceable, while knowledge remains distributed among the people and communities who maintain it.

**The goal is to preserve human effort and connect what people know, so progress can build on existing work instead of repeatedly starting over.**

## Experimental Record, Related Work, and Attribution

This record documents what was tried, why it was tried, what was built and used, what was observed, what worked, what failed, and what the results may mean. It includes experiments, observations, experiences, and interpretations, along with the code, measurements, decisions, and history behind them.

Some material may be wrong, redundant, impractical, or completely useless. Some may be valuable, lead to new discoveries, or confirm something already known. Nothing here is presented as the best approach, the first discovery, or the definitive answer to anything.

The work is preserved as it happened, including mistakes, dead ends, and unexpected results, so interested people can explore it, question it, reproduce it, or take it in another direction.

Research and development build upon generations of work by others. This record may include original developments, improvements to established methods, independent rediscovery, or approaches that overlap with existing research and projects. Novelty, priority, and recognition are not prerequisites for useful work.

A good-faith effort is made to identify and acknowledge relevant publications, projects, tools, and contributors, and to respect applicable licenses and attribution requirements. Some related work may become known only after research or development has already taken place. Citation as related work does not necessarily mean that it influenced or was used in the original implementation. Where external work is directly incorporated or adapted, the relevant source and credit should be identified.

No individual or project can reasonably guarantee awareness of every related idea, publication, implementation, or independent discovery. References and acknowledgments reflect the information available at the time and may be expanded or corrected as additional work comes to light. Similarity or overlap alone should not be interpreted as evidence of copying, misappropriation, or historical priority.

Constructive corrections, additional references, and contributions that improve the accuracy and usefulness of the published record are welcome.

**What others find useful, if anything, is entirely up to them.**

## Where This Is Going

Make Your Own World begins with independently useful open-source tools and research. Over time, the work can grow into interoperable infrastructure that helps people preserve, connect, and build on knowledge across systems and generations.

Success is practical: people can pursue meaningful work, retain its context, and share it on their own terms. The organization’s role is to make that easier through usable tools, clear evidence, and open collaboration.
